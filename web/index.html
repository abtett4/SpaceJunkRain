        What the marks mean. An amber trace follows a reference orbit built from the object’s real inclination and perigee/apogee; its direction and phase are illustrative, and it does not model the descent or claim a reentry location. For most objects that orbit is the one GCAT records for it, dated and often from its working life rather than its last days, so the card says when it was recorded. Tiangong-1 at short windows replays its last known orbit (SGP4). A ring marks an object with no usable orbit, at a stable random point on the globe chosen evenly by area — not a reentry location. Launches are every orbital launch attempt in GCAT, shown as a ring at the launch pad (at the launch site when GCAT has no pad coordinates; at the drop zone for air launches); failed attempts are smaller and red. Successful launches also get an illustrative ascent: a nine-minute arc from the pad onto the orbit GCAT records for that launch (or for the upper stage or a payload it left in orbit). The arc passes over the real pad at the real time with the orbit’s real tilt, so its plane is roughly right; it heads south for retrograde orbits and Vandenberg polar launches and north otherwise. It is not a flight reconstruction. Faint full orbits are drawn for active objects (Appearance can turn them off).

        Times. About 4,100 reentries have a time of day from GCAT, used only when GCAT and Space-Track agree on the day. The rest give only the day. Those objects get a stable, randomly assigned time within that UTC day; it is an animation anchor, not an estimated reentry time. Launch times keep GCAT’s precision, usually to the minute or second; the few given only to the day get an assigned time the same way.

        About the data

        • Decay dates come from the SATCAT and Space-Track's historical decay messages, which record each one to the day only. Each dot sits on its reported day. When only the date is known, a reproducible random time within that UTC day drives the timeline and orbit replay; it is not an estimated reentry time. Within a row, dots are spread up and down only so they don't overlap.
        • A "source" is everything from one launch (its international designator, e.g. 1999-025). Debris usually keeps its parent's designator, so a breakup shows up as a burst within one source.
        • Reentries bunch up around solar maxima: a more active Sun heats and expands the upper atmosphere, so drag pulls objects down faster. NASA's history of the problem records the 1978–80 and 1989–91 maxima doing exactly this, from Skylab's early fall to the quick decay of the Solwind debris (Portree & Loftus, Orbital Debris: A Chronology, NASA/TP-1999-208856, 1999).
        • "Still in orbit" counts every tracked object, working satellites included. Only tracked objects appear here, roughly softball-sized and up in low orbit. Collision dates and altitudes are hand-curated.
